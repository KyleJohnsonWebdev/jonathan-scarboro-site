Who I am as a person and therapist

When I'm looking at therapist websites and profiles myself, I find it very hard to know which ones I'll vibe with, which ones are skilled, and which ones are poor fits and/or kooks. So I'll try to say who I am and what I do in plain language.

I came of age in punk and skateboard culture in the '90s, got an art degree, and had a first career as a bicycle mechanic and shop owner. I love drawing and making things. I'm squarely middle-aged, queer, and a later-in-life parent. Although I don't talk much about myself in session, my life outside therapy influences my values and how I'll show up with you.

If you come to see me, I won't try to "fix" you or correct you, and I'm not going to hand you a stack of worksheets or tell you how to live your life. I will help you stop organizing your life around fear, shame, and habits. And if your old tools are dull, I'll help you build sharp new ones. We'll probably work well together if you value humor, honesty, and a straightforward, no-BS approach to growth.

Why do therapy?

Fair question. As I practice it, therapy is slow, expensive, and often painful. And, of course, there's no guarantee it'll help. So why bother? Why not just keep doing what you're doing?

What I have to say in reply is more of a story than an answer.

I was in my early thirties, running a small but successful bicycle shop with a friend. I'd just gotten married and was living on a beautiful half-acre in the city, with a small halfpipe in the barn out back. I had dogs. From the outside, things were going well. More than that, I'd built a life that was uniquely mine. I hadn't followed anyone else's script; I'd made it myself.

But things were getting weird. I didn't know how to talk to my then-wife or business partner when something wasn't working for me. I felt increasingly off and trapped by the circumstances of the life I'd built. Instead of dealing with the problems I was having with them, I found ways to ignore them: working harder, keeping to myself, drinking, and so on.

I finally went to therapy after my marriage and business took a crash landing. My therapist helped me talk about things I'd rather have forgotten and get to know parts of myself it would have been convenient not to know about. I had to confront who I actually was, rather than the person I'd constructed out of other people's ideas for me and about me.

It's been more than a decade since I finally decided to talk to someone about what was going on inside. It remains one of the most important decisions I've made for myself.

I can't promise it'll be worth it for you. But if you're curious about what might change when you stop trying to figure everything out on your own, that's a good place to start.
